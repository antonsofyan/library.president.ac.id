Cikarang, August 20, 2026 – The President University Library hosted an educational session regarding parenting approaches and challenges related to children's eye health in the digital age. The event featured two medical specialists: Pediatrician Dr. Theresia Santi, Sp.A, and Ophthalmologist Dr. Andreas Surya Anugrah Sinaga, Sp.M, FICS, AIFO-K. Titled “Literasi sebagai penjaga screentime anak di era digital” (Literacy as a Guardian of Children's Screen Time in the Digital Era), the seminar generally addressed how excessive screen time on gadgets can impact not only visual development but also physical growth and development.

In the first session, Dr. Theresia Santi, Sp.A, emphasized the importance of monitoring child development during the "golden age" amidst the pervasive influence of digital technology. She explained that rapid brain development occurs from infancy through the toddler years; the formation of neural networks relies on three key pillars: balanced nutrition, appropriate psychomotor stimulation, and protection through routine immunizations and growth monitoring. Uncontrolled gadget use during this age range raises concerns that these essential pillars might be compromised.

Furthermore, Dr. Theresia shared guidelines on healthy physical stimulation and social interaction for children. Rather than allowing children to engage in passive activities in front of screens, parents were encouraged to optimize sensory and fine motor stimulation through hands-on physical activities. Activities such as playing with kinetic sand, stacking blocks, cutting paper, and playing with modeling clay are far more effective at stimulating neural connections in the brain and fostering children's focus and social skills.

In the second session, Dr. Andreas Surya Anugrah Sinaga, Sp.M, FICS, AIFO-K, examined the impact of screen exposure on children's eyes from an ophthalmological perspective. He highlighted the fact that children aged 5 to 15 currently spend an average of 4 to 7 hours a day staring at electronic screens—far exceeding the medically recommended limit of less than 2 hours per day. This excessive exposure carries the risk of triggering various eye conditions, such as Computer Vision Syndrome, dry eyes, and refractive errors like myopia (nearsightedness).

Dr. Andreas also compared reading on digital screens with reading printed materials. Although both activities involve "near visual work" that strains eye muscles, digital screens pose a significantly higher risk of eye strain. This is due to glare, fluctuating contrast levels, and a drastic reduction in blinking frequency when children stare at device screens.

As a solution, both speakers agreed that fostering a culture of reading print—facilitated by libraries—could be a primary alternative for reducing children's screen time. Printed books are considered gentler on children's eyes and support deeper reading focus, free from the distracting, excessive visual stimulation found in digital media. Nevertheless, reading printed books should still be balanced with proper viewing distances, adequate room lighting, and regular breaks.

The seminar concluded with the sharing of practical tips for protecting children's eyes, including age-appropriate screen time limits, the "20-20-20 rule," taking breaks from near-vision activities every 40 minutes, and education on blinking exercises and regular eye check-ups. Through this seminar, the library hopes to continue serving as an educational hub and partner for parents and educators, guiding children toward healthier lifestyles and better literacy habits in the digital age.(Laurensius Yoel Fabian)
